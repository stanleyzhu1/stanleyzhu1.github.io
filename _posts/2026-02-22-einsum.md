The shorthand turns out to be `einsum`, and once the two rules click everything in the diagram above becomes a one-liner.

## `einsum`'s two rules

`einsum` ("Einstein summation") is a single string of index labels that fully describes a tensor operation. The whole language is two rules:

0. **A label that appears in more than one input is *contracted*** — the corresponding axes are matched and multiplied pointwise. 
2. **A label that appears in the inputs but not in the output is *summed* over.** In the classical Einstein convention, such a repeated label is called a *dummy index*.

Anything left — labels that appear in some input *and* in the output — is a free index that's broadcast through.

## Matrix multiplication as the warm-up

Plain matmul is the cleanest case:

```python
np.einsum('ij,jk->ik', A, B)
```

- `A` is labeled `ij`, `B` is labeled `jk`. Two letters per matrix because they're 2-D.
- Output is `ik`. So `j` appears in both inputs and is missing from the output.
- Rule 1: align and multiply on `j`. Rule 2: sum over `j`. That's exactly $C_{ik} = \sum_j A_{ij} B_{jk}$.

`i` and `k` are free indices — each $(i, k)$ pair gets its own scalar in the output.

## Back to BTD × DNH → BTNH

Now the puzzle:

```python
Q = einsum('btd,dnh->btnh', X, W_Q)
```

Using the diagram's legend:

- **B** = batch, **T** = tokens, **D** = $d_{\text{model}}$
- **N** = number of heads, **H** = per-head dimension

Walk through the rules:

- `d` appears in both inputs (`btd` and `dnh`), missing from output. Rules 1 + 2: align on `d`, multiply, sum. The embedding axis gets contracted exactly the way `j` did in plain matmul.
- `b`, `t`, `n`, `h` each appear in some input *and* in the output. Free indices, broadcast through.

Result: $Q_{btnh} = \sum_d X_{btd} (W_Q)_{dnh}$.

If you flatten this back to 2-D, $W_Q$ has shape $(D, NH)$ and the product is $(B, T, NH)$ — the same matmul you'd write with `@`. The 4-D einsum just writes $N$ and $H$ as separate axes from the start, instead of doing the matmul into $NH$ and reshaping after. Either gives the same bytes; the einsum form is what people put in code because it spells out the per-head structure without an explicit reshape.

The arrow in the diagram is doing the contraction over `d` *and* the head-split — both at once, in five letters of input plus four of output.

## Cheat sheet

A handful of common operations, framed as "what's the einsum?".

**Problem 1: Sum every element of a 1-D vector.**

<details markdown="1">
<summary><i>Click here for the answer.</i></summary>

`'i->'`

`i` is in the input, missing from the output → summed.

</details>

**Problem 2: Dot product of two vectors.**

<details markdown="1">
<summary><i>Click here for the answer.</i></summary>

`'i,i->'`

Both inputs labeled `i` → align and multiply (rule 1). `i` not in output → sum (rule 2). That's $\sum_i a_i b_i$.

</details>

**Problem 3: Outer product of two vectors.**

<details markdown="1">
<summary><i>Click here for the answer.</i></summary>

`'i,j->ij'`

Inputs use *different* letters, so nothing is contracted. Both indices appear in the output, so every $(i, j)$ pair gets its own entry: $a_i b_j$.

</details>

**Problem 4: Trace of a square matrix.**

<details markdown="1">
<summary><i>Click here for the answer.</i></summary>

`'ii->'`

Repeating `i` *within a single input* picks the diagonal. Then `i` is missing from the output, so we sum the diagonal.

</details>

**Problem 5: Transpose.**

<details markdown="1">
<summary><i>Click here for the answer.</i></summary>

`'ij->ji'`

No contraction, no sum — just a relabel of axes.

</details>

**Problem 6: Matrix–vector product.**

<details markdown="1">
<summary><i>Click here for the answer.</i></summary>

`'ij,j->i'`

`j` appears in both inputs and is dropped from the output → contract on `j`.

</details>

**Problem 7: Batched matmul.**

<details markdown="1">
<summary><i>Click here for the answer.</i></summary>

`'bij,bjk->bik'`

`b` is a free batch dimension carried through both inputs. `j` contracts, just like in plain matmul.

</details>

**Problem 8: Attention scores from $Q$ and $K$.**

<details markdown="1">
<summary><i>Click here for the answer.</i></summary>

`'bhqd,bhkd->bhqk'`

$Q$ is `(batch, heads, seq_q, dim)`; $K$ is `(batch, heads, seq_k, dim)`. The embedding axis `d` contracts; `b` and `h` pass through; `q` and `k` are free.

</details>

## A note on the name

Einstein introduced the convention in 1916 to drop the explicit $\sum$ whenever an index repeats. NumPy's `einsum` (2011) generalizes the idea — the explicit `->output` half lets one string express contractions, broadcasts, and reshapes.
